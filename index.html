Kaushik Mohan

SCSS Wednesday June 10 · 9:05 AM – 9:25 AM

The Language of Sequence Analysis

State Sequence Analysis (SSA), used in the social sciences for longitudinal and life-course research, is limited by simpler probabilistic models and non-generative non-parametric models. Our broader research explores the use of generative Transformer architectures, the structural foundation of Large Language Models (LLMs), to address the unique constraints of categorical sequence analysis. Current work evaluates the efficacy of training small Transformer models for sequence imputation and representation learning.

Muskan Walia

Wednesday June 10 · 9:25 AM – 9:45 AM SCSS

Stress-testing Generative AI in High-Stakes Policy Settings: Evidence from Race-Blind Charging in California

Generative AI systems are rapidly moving from prototypes to embedded components of government decision-making infrastructure. For instance, California mandates all prosecutors implement a new review procedure called “race-blind charging”, where prosecutors review case documents with race-related information redacted. To make this feasible, the state encouraged prosecutors to use AI-based redaction. We designed and tested one such system that uses generative AI to automatically redact race-related information from police reports. We present these validation results, demonstrating that our algorithm reliably removes all race-related indicators required by law, reduces the ability to predict an arrestee’s race from redacted narratives, and performs at the top of its class relative to existing alternatives.

Jawed Nazari

Wednesday June 10 · 9:50 AM – 10:10 AM Int'l Ed

Member-Level Integration of Refugee Families in the United States: A Qualitative Multiple-case Study of Afghan Families Resettled After 2021

This study examines how refugee family integration unfolds unevenly within the same household. Focusing on Afghan families who arrived in the United States after 2021, it asks how spouses and children experience and negotiate life differently as they encounter schools, workplaces, service agencies, and legal systems. The study is guided by four mechanisms: structural conditions and institutional encounters, gender-order renegotiation, intergenerational timing gaps and youth brokering, and family systems and identity/belonging. Using a qualitative multiple-case study of Afghan families in New York and New Jersey, the study offers a member-level framework showing how integration is produced through everyday household negotiation.

Abdul Hamid Hatsaandh

Wednesday June 10 · 10:10 AM – 10:30 AM Int'l Ed

EdTech for Whom? Heterogeneous Effects of Educational Technology in Secondary Education

The evidence base on EdTech effectiveness has grown rapidly, yet the dominant finding in the literature remains one of average effects, estimates that pool across students and obscure whether all learners benefit equally or whether gains accrue disproportionately to those already advantaged. The question of who benefits, and whether the evidence can credibly answer it, is the organizing concern of this dissertation: what do experimental and quasi-experimental evaluations reveal about heterogeneous learning effects of EdTech among secondary school learners, and what do the statistical properties of those evaluations reveal about the reliability of their subgroup findings? The dissertation addresses this through three papers. The first is a systematic review of experimental and quasi-experimental EdTech evaluations at the secondary school level, which synthesizes reported heterogeneous effects and conducts a power audit, calculating the minimum detectable interaction effect for each included study to document what individual evaluations could and could not have credibly detected given their sample sizes. The second paper examines these dynamics empirically in Afghanistan, where a broadcast-based EdTech intervention is evaluated for its differential effects across structural moderators including gender, geographic location, and socioeconomic status. The third paper addresses the power problem directly: using a subset of RCTs identified through the systematic review and selected for sample size, subgroup reporting, and microdata availability, it pools individual-level data to conduct a properly powered analysis of heterogeneous effects.

Christopher Buglino

Wednesday June 10 · 10:35 AM – 10:55 AM Data Science/Poly

Single-cell quantification of CAR-T tumor killing efficiency: A BART-based model of functional heterogeneity

Chimeric Antigen Receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy has emerged as a novel and effective form of personalized cancer therapy, however, heterogeneous CAR-T subpopulations have differing tumor-killing efficiencies and result in a high variance of clinical health outcomes. Characterization of CAR-T heterogeneity is hindered by the need to observe live, single-cell interactions and multiplexed protein secretions simultaneously. Using a CAR-T / tumor co-culture dataset collected by a novel single-cell measurement platform, I developed a probabilistic model of CAR-T tumor killing efficiency as a function of time-varying cell motion, and secretions of tumor necrosis factor alpha, interferon gamma, and granzyme B. The proposed killing efficiency index identifies CAR-T functional behaviors including immune cells that contact tumors without killing them and immune cells with high serial-killing potential. Internal model validity was performed by functional decomposition to verify consistency with known tumor-killing mechanisms.

George Perrett

Wednesday June 10 · 10:55 AM – 11:15 AM SCSS

Flaws in the LLM Automation Narrative

Large Language Models are frequently advertised as operating at the level of human experts, and the prospect of automating knowledge economy work is offered as justification for the high cost of developing and deploying them. Using a novel benchmark design that explicitly measures response variance and error magnitude, we show that the output of a frontier Large Language Model on a computer coding task is highly variable and frequently contains extreme errors. These findings undermine the automation narrative and demonstrate the importance of considering variance and error magnitude in benchmark evaluations.
